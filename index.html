Day 1 — Wednesday, 25 November
Arrival · Haneda · one easy evening, nothing more
Before you fly
Two things done at home, not at the airport

Both take ten minutes and both save you a queue.

  1. Visit Japan Web — register the trip, get the immigration and customs QR codes, screenshot them. Singapore passports are visa-free for 90 days, but the QR is what turns a 25-minute arrivals queue into a 5-minute one.
  2. Add Suica to Apple Wallet — Wallet → + → Transit Card → Suica. Top up ¥3,000 with a card before you leave. You then walk off the plane and straight through a ticket gate with your phone, no machine, no cash, no card to buy.
Do the Suica one at home on wifi. Setting it up in an arrivals hall on airport wifi is exactly the wrong moment.

Day 2 — Thursday, 26 November
Meiji Jingū → Harajuku → the ginkgo avenue → Shibuya at sunset
07:30
Meiji Jingū 明治神宮

Opens at sunrise and closes at sunset — which in November means roughly 06:30 to 16:00. Go early and you get a 70-hectare forest in the middle of Tokyo almost to yourself.

Enter through the giant cypress torii at the Harajuku end and walk the gravel path in. Bow once at the torii, keep to the sides of the path rather than the centre, and at the purification basin: rinse left hand, right hand, then pour into your left hand to rinse your mouth. Nobody minds if you get it slightly wrong.

Harajuku — but skip most of Takeshita Street

Walk Takeshita-dōri once, for about ten minutes, because you should see it. Then leave. The good Harajuku is Cat Street, the pedestrian lane running south — independent shops, vintage, coffee, and space to actually walk.

Takeshita is unbearable after about 11am and on any weekend. This is a weekday morning, which is the only good time it has.

Omotesandō 表参道 — the architecture walk

The broad zelkova-lined avenue is a free open-air museum of contemporary architecture: Herzog & de Meuron's Prada crystal, SANAA's Dior, Tadao Ando's Omotesandō Hills, Kengo Kuma's Sunny Hills a few streets back. You do not need to go into any of them.

Lunch nearby: Maisen for tonkatsu in a converted bathhouse, or Afuri for yuzu-shio ramen if you want something lighter.

Icho Namiki 銀杏並木 — the ginkgo tunnel

This is the single most seasonal thing you can do on this trip, and you are here in the exact fortnight it works.

Roughly 150 ginkgo trees line the avenue at Meiji Jingū Gaien, and in the last week of November they go completely gold, with the fallen leaves making a yellow carpet underneath. There is usually a food-stall festival running alongside it. It is fifteen minutes from Omotesandō on foot.

Peak is a moving target — usually late November into the first week of December. Search the avenue on Instagram or a foliage tracker the morning of, and if it is a week early, Rikugien or Koishikawa Kōrakuen gardens are the fallback.

Day 3 — Friday, 27 November
Out of the city · a weekday, which is the right day for this
The choice
One day trip, four candidates

Friday is deliberately the day out: everywhere on this list is punishing on a weekend in foliage season, and merely busy on a Friday.

The full plan for each option — trains, passes, costs, and how the day actually runs — is on the Day Trips tab. The short version:

Kamakura & Enoshima
~55 min · ~¥2,000 return · easiest

Coastal temple town. Late November is exactly when Hase-dera and the Kita-Kamakura temples turn. Ends with the Enoden tram along the sea and Fuji on the horizon at sunset.

Hakone
~85 min · ~¥6,100 pass · biggest day

Mountains, a volcanic valley, a lake, and an onsen. The most spectacular option and the most tiring — and the ropeway sometimes shuts for volcanic gas.

Kawaguchiko
~2h bus · ~¥4,400 return · all about Fuji

The postcard: Chūreitō pagoda with Fuji behind it. Winter is the clearest season for the mountain, but buses sell out and it is a long ride.

Nikkō
~2h · ~¥6,000 return · the long one

Extravagantly carved shrines in cedar forest. Genuinely stunning, but the famous mountain foliage is finished by late November and it eats the whole day.

If you only want one recommendation: Kamakura. It is the shortest journey, the cheapest, the best-timed for late-November colour, and it is the only one that ends with the sea.
Day tripDecide by Wed
Whatever you pick
Three rules for a day out of Tokyo
  1. Leave before 08:00. Sunset at 16:30 means a 10am start gives you five usable hours, not eight.
  2. Carry cash. Small temples, buses, and country-town restaurants are far more cash-only than central Tokyo. ¥10,000 in notes covers the day comfortably.
  3. Take the layer. Every one of these is colder than the city — Hakone by 5°C, Kamakura by sea wind, Nikkō by altitude.

Day 4 — Saturday, 28 November
Old Tokyo · Asakusa → Kappabashi → Ueno → Yanaka
07:00
Sensō-ji 浅草寺 before it wakes up

This is a Saturday, and by ten o'clock Nakamise-dōri is shoulder to shoulder. At seven it is empty, the lanterns are still lit, and the whole thing is yours.

The temple grounds never close. Only the shops on Nakamise open late — around 09:00 — which is the point: walk the approach in silence first, come back for the shops after. Draw an omikuji fortune for ¥100; if you get a bad one, tie it to the rack and leave it behind.

Kappabashi かっぱ橋道具街 — kitchen town

Eight hundred metres of shops selling nothing but restaurant supply: knives, ceramics, copper pans, and the plastic food models you see in restaurant windows. It is the best souvenir street in Tokyo and almost nobody outside Japan knows it exists.

Kamata Hakensha for knives (they will engrave your name), Union Commerce and the ceramic shops for bowls, Maizuru for the fake food.

A carbon-steel knife will rust if you leave it wet — ask for stainless unless you want the maintenance. And declare it as checked luggage, obviously.

Everyone knows Ueno for cherry blossom. Late November is the other half of its year: ginkgo and maple around Shinobazu Pond, and far fewer people.

Yanaka 谷中 at golden hour

One of the very few parts of Tokyo that survived both the 1923 earthquake and the 1945 firebombing, so it still has wooden houses, narrow lanes and an enormous cemetery full of cats. Yūyake Dandan — literally "sunset stairs" — drops down into Yanaka Ginza, a single shopping street of croquette stands and old shops, and it faces west. Be standing at the top of those stairs at about 16:15.

West side · Shimokitazawa → Nakameguro → Daikanyama → illuminations
10:00
Shimokitazawa 下北沢

Low-rise, car-free, and stuffed with second-hand clothing, record shops, tiny theatres and coffee. Sunday is its best day — everything is open and the street is full.

There is no route to follow. Get off at Shimo-Kitazawa on the Keiō Inokashira line, pick a direction, and let the lanes take you. Vintage worth the time: New York Joe Exchange in an old bathhouse, Flamingo, Chicago, Haight & Ashbury for the properly curated stuff. Coffee at Bear Pond Espresso — small, serious, no photos of the bar.

Nakameguro 中目黒 — the canal

The Meguro River is famous in April for cherry blossom; in late November the same trees are red and there is nobody there. Walk the canal, cross the little bridges, and drop into the shops that line it — Onibus Coffee in a tiny house next to the train tracks, Sidewalk Stand, Cow Books for the shelf of it.

📍 Nakameguro canal
15:00
Daikanyama 代官山 — T-Site

Fifteen minutes uphill from Nakameguro. Daikanyama T-Site is three linked pavilions of bookshop, wrapped in a lattice of interlocking Ts, with a magazine section that runs to thousands of international titles and an upstairs lounge you can sit and read in. It is the nicest bookshop most people will ever walk into and it is free to wander.

The surrounding streets are quiet, low-rise and expensive, which after two days of Shibuya is exactly the point.

📍 Daikanyama T-Site
17:00
Winter illuminations — the reason the dark is fine

Tokyo answers a 16:30 sunset by lighting the entire city. The illuminations switch on from mid-November and are running by the time you are here.

  • Yebisu Garden Place — one enormous Baccarat chandelier in the plaza, ten minutes from Daikanyama. The obvious one for tonight.
  • Marunouchi Naka-dōri — a kilometre of champagne-gold trees between Tokyo Station and the palace.
  • Roppongi Keyakizaka — blue-and-white avenue framing Tokyo Tower at the end of it.
  • Shibuya Ao no Dōkutsu — the "blue cave" tunnel of light in Yoyogi Park, when it runs.
Exact start dates shift each year. Check one listing site in the week before you fly rather than trusting last year's dates.

Day 6 — Monday, 30 November
Breakfast, the last shopping, and out
07:00
Tsukiji Outer Market 築地場外市場

The wholesale auction moved to Toyosu in 2018, but the outer market — the four hundred-odd stalls and small restaurants — stayed exactly where it was, and that is the part you actually want.

Eat standing up and eat several things: tamagoyaki on a stick, grilled scallop, uni, a tuna skewer, strawberry daifuku. Most stalls open around 07:00 and start shutting by early afternoon.

The last haul

Ginza is ten minutes' walk from Tsukiji, which makes the morning easy: Muji Ginza flagship, Itōya for stationery across nine floors, Uniqlo Ginza. Or go the other way entirely and do a Don Quijote run for snacks, skincare and Kit Kat flavours, which is objectively the better souvenir per yen.

Check the tax-free rule before you shop. Japan has been moving from tax-free-at-the-till to buy-with-tax and claim-a-refund-at-the-airport, with the change flagged for around November 2026. Either way: bring your passport, keep every receipt, and leave time at Haneda for the refund counter — it is before security.

Bags
Don't carry the suitcase around all morning

Two options, both cheap. Leave it with the hotel after check-out and come back for it — free, and the reason to keep the morning local. Or use a coin locker at any major station; the large ones take a case and run about ¥700 a day, and the Coin Locker Navi app shows what is free.

Takkyūbin — Japan's overnight luggage courier — is the local trick, but it is for hotel-to-hotel. For an airport run on the same day it does not help.

🏨 Where to Stay

Five nights, one hotel — the neighbourhood matters more than the room

All four of these are fine. The real question is what you want at the end of a long day: to fall out of a station into a wall of neon, or to walk a quiet street. Anywhere on the Yamanote loop line is well-connected enough — that is the ring line that most of Tokyo hangs off.
Book early, because a room for three is the hard part. Most Tokyo hotel rooms are doubles or twins and cap at two people. Triples exist but they are a small share of the stock and they go first in autumn. Three realistic shapes, in rough order of value:
  • One triple room — cheapest per head and you are all in one place. Search specifically for "triple", or set the guest count to 3 before you filter anything else; it is the only way to see real availability.
  • An apartment or aparthotel — MIMARU, the serviced-apartment brands and most Tokyo Airbnbs are built for exactly this, usually come with a kitchenette and a washing machine, and often land cheaper than two hotel rooms.
  • A twin plus a single — most available, most expensive, and you lose the shared base. Worth it only if the first two draw a blank.
Whatever you book, put all three names on the reservation. Japanese hotels register every guest at check-in, and an undeclared third person is a real problem at the desk rather than a shrug.

💡 How Japan Actually Works

The things that catch people out, and the handful that genuinely matter

Japan is the easiest hard country to travel in. Almost nothing here is dangerous, dirty, or a scam. What it is instead is particular — there are settled ways of doing ordinary things, and nobody will tell you when you get one wrong, they will just quietly absorb it. Learning about six of them is the difference between visiting and fitting in. The rest of this page is those six, plus the practical stuff.

💴 Money — Less Cash Than You Think, More Than You Have

Card acceptance has transformed; it has not finished

✅ Card works fine
  • Every konbini, department store, chain restaurant and hotel
  • All trains and buses — via the Suica, which you top up by card
  • Big shops, Don Quijote, Uniqlo, Muji, electronics
  • Most cafes in Shibuya, Shinjuku, Ginza, Omotesandō
  • Taxis, almost universally now
💴 Bring cash for
  • Temples and shrines — entry fees, ¥300–¥600, cash only, exact-ish
  • Ramen vending machines — many take notes only
  • Small izakaya and yokochō bars — assume cash
  • Tsukiji and market stalls
  • Older shops in Shimokitazawa, Yanaka, Kappabashi
  • Coin lockers — though newer ones take IC cards
Where to get yen: the ATMs inside 7-Eleven take foreign cards, are everywhere, work 24 hours and have an English menu. Japan Post Bank ATMs are the backup. Bank ATMs often refuse foreign cards outright. Carry ¥15,000–20,000 and top up as it goes — you will be surprised how slowly it does. And do not tip. Ever. Not in restaurants, not in taxis, not for housekeeping. Leaving money on a table means you forgot it, and someone will chase you down the street to give it back.

🚉 Six Things People Get Wrong

Nobody will correct you, so here they are

🤫
1 · Trains are quiet carriages, all of them ▸
  • No phone calls on trains. None. Put the phone on silent — the local phrase for it is "manner mode" and every announcement asks for it.
  • Talk quietly if you talk at all. A normal-volume conversation in English is audible in a way it never is at home.
  • Backpack off and held in front of you, or on the rack, when it is busy.
  • No eating on commuter trains. It is fine on long-distance and shinkansen — that is what the bento is for.
  • Priority seats near the doors: leave them, and switch your phone off entirely near them if you want to be properly correct.
🚶
2 · Don't eat while walking, and there are no bins ▸
  • Buy the street food, then stand next to the stall and eat it. Most stalls have a small shelf or a bin for exactly this. Walking along with a skewer in your hand reads as slightly rude, and in Kamakura and Asakusa there are signs asking you not to.
  • Public bins essentially do not exist — a legacy of the 1995 subway attack. Carry a small plastic bag for your own rubbish.
  • Where bins do exist: inside konbini (by the door), on station platforms, and beside vending machines for the can or bottle you just bought.
  • Rubbish is separated — burnable, plastic, cans, bottles. The pictures on the lids are clear enough.
  • Despite all this, the streets are spotless. That is not because it is cleaned constantly; it is because everyone carries their rubbish home.
🍜
3 · How to order in a small restaurant ▸
  • Ramen shops: the machine at the door is the till. Put in cash, press the button for what you want (top-left is usually the house special), take the ticket, sit, hand it over. Do this before you sit down.
  • Wait to be seated almost everywhere else, even when it looks empty. Hold up fingers for the number of people.
  • The hot towel — oshibori — is for your hands, not your face.
  • Slurping noodles is fine and cools them. Do not stick chopsticks upright in rice, and do not pass food chopstick-to-chopstick — both are funeral rites.
  • Pour drinks for the other person, not yourself, and let them pour yours.
  • Pay at the till on the way out, not at the table. There is usually a small tray for the money — put the notes in the tray rather than into a hand.
  • "Gochisōsama deshita" on the way out. It means "that was a feast" and it will make someone's shift.
♨️
4 · Onsen and sentō, if you go ▸
  • You wash first, thoroughly, sitting on a stool at the taps, before you get anywhere near the bath. The bath is for soaking in clean, not for washing.
  • No swimwear. Public baths are nude and gender-separated. The small towel you are given is for modesty walking around; it does not go in the water — rest it on your head or the side.
  • Tie long hair up. Don't swim, splash or take a phone in.
  • Tattoos are still refused at many places. Some are relaxed, some allow cover patches, and the reliable answer is a kashikiri private bath booked by the hour — Hakone Yuryō is set up for exactly this.
  • Drink water afterwards, and don't go straight in after a big meal or a lot of alcohol.
⛩️
5 · Shrines and temples, in thirty seconds ▸
  • Shrine = Shinto (torii gate at the entrance). Temple = Buddhist (usually a large gate with guardian statues, and an incense burner). Most Japanese people use both without thinking about it.
  • Bow once at the torii and walk to the side of the path — the centre is left for the gods.
  • At the water basin: left hand, right hand, pour into the left hand to rinse your mouth, then tip the ladle up to rinse the handle. Do not drink from the ladle.
  • At a shrine: coin in the box, bow twice, clap twice, hands together, bow once. At a temple: the same but no clapping — just bow, hands together, bow.
  • A ¥5 coin is the traditional offering because the word for it sounds like "good fortune".
  • Photographs are usually fine outdoors, usually not inside the main hall. Look for the sign; there always is one.
🧾
6 · Tax-free shopping — and the rule that is changing ▸
  • The long-standing rule: spend ¥5,000+ in one shop in one day, show your passport at the tax-free counter, and the 10% consumption tax comes off at the till.
  • This is being replaced by a refund-at-departure system, announced for around November 2026 — you pay the full price in the shop and claim the refund at the airport instead. Check which system is live before you go shopping, because it changes what you do on the last morning.
  • Either way: carry your passport when you shop, and keep every receipt together in one place.
  • Leave a real buffer at Haneda. The refund desks are before security, and the queue is worst in the morning.
  • Consumables bought tax-free are sealed in a bag you are not meant to open until you leave the country. In practice nobody checks, but the seal is there for a reason.

🍁 This Specific Week

Late November has its own rules

  • It is dark at half four. This is the single biggest planning constraint on the trip. Shrines, gardens and viewpoints close at or near sunset — Meiji Jingū shuts around 16:00, Shinjuku Gyoen earlier still. Do outdoor things in the morning and let the evening be food and lights.
  • Autumn colour is a moving target. Central Tokyo — Icho Namiki, Rikugien, Koishikawa Kōrakuen, Ueno — peaks in the last week of November into the first week of December, so you are well placed. The mountains, Hakone and Nikkō included, peaked a month ago. Check a foliage tracker in the week before you fly.
  • Garden light-ups. Rikugien and several other gardens run evening illuminations of the maples in late November, with extended hours. They are ticketed and they are lovely, and they solve the early-sunset problem neatly.
  • Winter illuminations are on from mid-November — Marunouchi, Roppongi, Yebisu, Shibuya. Free, and the reason a 16:30 sunset stops mattering.
  • Mon 23 November is Labour Thanksgiving Day, a Japanese public holiday. You land two days after it, so the domestic-travel crush will have cleared, but the preceding weekend is one of the busiest of the year at every foliage spot.
  • Indoor heating is aggressive. Trains, shops and restaurants are hot. Dress in layers you can peel off, not one thick coat you have to carry.
  • The air is very dry. Lip balm and moisturiser are not optional, and a hotel room will dry your throat out overnight — most have a humidifier setting or a small one in the cupboard.
